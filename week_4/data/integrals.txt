Integrals

An antiderivative of a function f is a function F such that F'(x) = f(x). The indefinite
integral of f, written as the integral of f(x) dx, represents the entire family of
antiderivatives of f, and always includes an arbitrary constant of integration, "+ C",
because two antiderivatives of the same function can only differ by a constant.

The definite integral of f(x) from a to b, written as the integral of f(x) dx from a to
b, represents the signed area between the curve y = f(x) and the x-axis over the
interval [a, b]. Area above the x-axis counts as positive, and area below counts as
negative.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects derivatives and integrals: it states that if
F is an antiderivative of f, then the definite integral of f(x) dx from a to b equals
F(b) - F(a). This is what makes it possible to evaluate definite integrals directly from
an antiderivative instead of computing an area numerically.

Common integration techniques include substitution (reversing the chain rule, useful when
the integrand contains a function and its derivative), integration by parts (reversing
the product rule, useful for products like x*e^x), and partial fraction decomposition
(useful for rational functions before integrating each simpler term separately).

Definite integrals also have practical applications beyond area: they can compute the
volume of a solid of revolution, the total displacement of an object from its velocity
function, and the average value of a function over an interval.
